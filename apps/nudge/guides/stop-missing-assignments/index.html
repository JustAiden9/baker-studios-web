How to stop missing assignments

A simple system for students: one list of every due date, a couple of well-timed reminders, and no guilt when something slips.

By the Nudge team at Baker Studios · Updated October 25, 2026

Short answer

Get every due date into one list automatically, so nothing depends on memory. Then set two reminders per deadline, one the day before and one a few hours before, keep them quiet overnight, break big work into small sessions with their own start dates, and mark work done the moment you submit it. If something slips anyway, hand it in late rather than not at all.

Nudge exists because of one missed assignment. It was right there on the Canvas list, but nothing tapped its maker on the shoulder in time. That’s the real problem for most students: not caring too little, but relying on a list you have to remember to check.

Here’s the simple system Nudge is built around. You can run it with any tools you like.

1. Put every due date in one place, automatically

Due dates live in different places: each class’s page, the syllabus, a teacher’s announcement. Copying them into a planner works for about two weeks. Instead, use your school platform’s calendar feed, one private link that carries every dated assignment from every class and updates itself. Here’s where to find it for Canvas, Schoology, Blackboard, Brightspace, Moodle and others.

2. Get reminded when you can still act

School notifications tell you when work is posted or graded. What you need is a tap at the moment you can still do something about it. Two reminders cover most work:

  • The day before: enough time to start, or to ask for help.
  • A few hours before: enough time to finish and submit.

If two things are due together, one reminder for both beats two in a row. More than that and you start swiping them away without reading.

3. Keep nights quiet

A reminder at 1 a.m. for something due at 8 a.m. doesn’t help anyone. Move late-night reminders to the evening before, while you can still do the work, and let your sleep be sleep.

4. Give big work its own start dates

An essay due in two weeks has one date on the calendar and none of the dates that matter: when to outline, when to draft, when to edit. Split it into short sessions and put each on your calendar. If one slips, re-plan the rest rather than giving up on the plan.

5. Mark work done when you submit it

Calendar feeds know due dates, not what you’ve turned in. Ticking off submitted work keeps your list honest, so what’s left is only what still needs you.

6. If something slips, hand it in anyway

A late assignment is usually worth more than a missing one. Check your syllabus’s late policy, send the work with a short note if you need to, and get back to what’s next. No streaks, no guilt.

Questions

Why do I keep missing assignments?

Usually not because you don’t care. Due dates are scattered across classes and platforms, school notifications arrive when work is posted rather than when you need to start, and a list only helps when you remember to look at it.

How many reminders should I get before a deadline?

Two or three is enough for most work: one the day before, so you can still start, and one a few hours before, so you can finish and submit. More than that turns into noise you learn to ignore.

What should I do if I’ve already missed an assignment?

Submit it anyway, as soon as you can. Many teachers accept late work for partial credit, and some for full credit with a short email. Check your syllabus, send the work, and move on to what’s next.
